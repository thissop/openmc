\section{Part 0 -- Why this matters}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Fusion in one slide}
  The deuterium--tritium (DT) reaction powers most fusion designs:
  \[
    \mathrm{D} + \mathrm{T} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{{}^4\mathrm{He}}_{3.5\ \mathrm{MeV}} \;+\; \underbrace{n}_{14.1\ \mathrm{MeV}}
  \]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The \keyword{alpha} (${}^4$He) is \emph{charged} $\Rightarrow$ trapped by the magnetic field $\Rightarrow$ heats the plasma.
    \item The \keyword{neutron} carries \alert{$\sim$80\% of the energy} and is \emph{uncharged} $\Rightarrow$ it is \alert{not confined} -- it flies straight out of the plasma.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Why you should care}
    All that neutron energy lands on the surrounding wall. Managing it -- and using it -- is one of the central engineering problems of a fusion reactor.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the escaping neutrons do}
  Those 14.1 MeV neutrons stream out and hit the \keyword{first wall} and \keyword{blanket}. They:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Heat} the structure (the power you want to extract).
    \item \keyword{Damage} materials (atoms knocked out of place $\to$ embrittlement; lifetime limit).
    \item \keyword{Breed tritium}: $n + {}^6\mathrm{Li}\to {}^4\mathrm{He}+\mathrm{T}$ in a Li blanket -- you must make your own T fuel.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{The central question of this work}
    \alert{Where do the neutrons hit the wall, and how intensely?} The answer drives cooling, material lifetime, and fuel self-sufficiency.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{``Neutron wall load'' (NWL): the key metric}
  \keyword{Neutron wall load} = neutron power (or current) arriving \alert{per unit area} of the first wall (e.g.\ MW/m$^2$).
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A \emph{spatial map}: some parts of the wall are hit harder than others.
    \item Engineers care about the \alert{peak}, not just the average: hot spots set the cooling requirement and the component lifetime.
    \item The \keyword{peaking factor} = peak / average. Lower (more uniform) is generally easier to engineer.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize In this work NWL is computed as a \alert{geometric} quantity (we will be precise about what that includes and excludes).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The idea: what if you could aim the neutrons?}
  Normally DT neutrons come off \keyword{isotropically} (equally in all directions). But if you \alert{align the nuclear spins} of the fuel, the emission becomes \keyword{directional}.
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{The payoff}
    \alert{Steer neutrons away from fragile, hard-to-replace parts} (the tokamak \keyword{center stack} / inboard magnets) and \alert{toward robust outer walls}. Same fusion power, gentler on the weak spot.
  \end{block}
  \vfill
  This deck is about \alert{modeling that steering} and \alert{verifying the model is correct} -- the prerequisite before anyone trusts it for design.
\end{frame}

\section{Part 1 -- Two ways to find where neutrons go}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two complementary methods}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
      \begin{block}{Analytic (paper-and-pencil)}
        \footnotesize Assume neutrons travel in \alert{straight lines} from birth to wall (no scattering). Then the wall load is a \alert{geometry integral} you can do in closed form.\\[4pt]
        $+$ instant, exact, differentiable\\
        $-$ only valid with \alert{no scattering}; simple shapes
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
      \begin{block}{Monte Carlo (simulation)}
        \footnotesize \alert{Simulate many individual neutrons} one at a time, using random numbers for each step; average the results.\\[4pt]
        $+$ handles scattering, real materials, any geometry\\
        $-$ slower; has \alert{statistical noise} ($\sim 1/\sqrt{N}$)
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize Schwartz (2025) provides the \alert{analytic} method. \keyword{OpenMC} is the \alert{Monte-Carlo} code. This project connects the two.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The key idea: ``recovery'' as a correctness check}
  In the special case of \alert{no scattering}, both methods describe the \emph{same} physics, so they must give the \alert{same answer}.
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{The validation logic (used throughout this work)}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Build the Monte-Carlo source and turn scattering \alert{off} (free-streaming).
      \item Check that OpenMC \alert{recovers} (reproduces, within statistical noise) the known analytic answer.
      \item Agreement $\Rightarrow$ the source and the simulation machinery are \alert{correct}.
      \item \alert{Only then} turn on the physics the analytic method \emph{cannot} do (scattering, blankets, real shapes).
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
  \vfill
  \alert{``Recovery'' is the heart of this report:} we earn trust in the new tool by first reproducing a known result.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why not just go straight to Monte Carlo?}
  Because a brand-new custom source could be \alert{subtly wrong} (a flipped sign, a bad rotation, a mis-normalized distribution) and still produce \emph{plausible-looking} output.
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The analytic result is an \alert{independent, exact} answer for the no-scattering case.
    \item Matching it to a fraction of a percent is strong evidence the MC source is right.
    \item It also calibrates the \alert{statistical noise}: residuals should look like random scatter, not a systematic bias.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize This is standard practice in computational physics: \alert{verify against a known solution} before trusting a code on the unknown.
\end{frame}

\section{Part 2 -- A neutronics \& OpenMC primer}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{What is Monte-Carlo particle transport?}
  Simulate the \keyword{life history} of one neutron at a time:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \keyword{Born} somewhere (position), heading some way (direction), with some energy.
    \item \keyword{Fly} in a straight line until it (maybe) interacts with a nucleus.
    \item \keyword{Interact}: scatter (change direction/energy), get absorbed, etc.
    \item Repeat until it leaks out or dies; \keyword{tally} what it did along the way.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
  Run \alert{millions} of histories; the averages converge to the physical answer, with error shrinking like $1/\sqrt{N}$.
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    It is a numerical experiment: instead of solving an equation, you ``play out'' many random neutron journeys and count outcomes.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The vocabulary you need (1/2)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Source} -- the rule for how neutrons are \alert{born}: their position, \alert{direction}, and energy. \emph{This is what we wrote.}
    \item \keyword{Cross section} -- how likely a neutron is to interact per unit path length. Large $\Rightarrow$ interacts often; near zero $\Rightarrow$ flies through.
    \item \keyword{Mean free path} -- average distance between interactions ($= 1/\text{cross section}$).
    \item \keyword{Void / vacuum} -- a region with $\approx$ zero cross section: the neutron \alert{free-streams} (straight line, no interactions).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{How we get ``no scattering'' in OpenMC}
    Make the interior a \alert{void}. Then the mean free path is huge compared to the device, so every neutron flies straight from birth to wall -- exactly the analytic assumption.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The vocabulary you need (2/2)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Tally} -- what you \alert{measure} during the simulation (a counter you accumulate).
    \item \keyword{Surface current} -- a tally that counts neutrons \alert{crossing a surface} (e.g.\ the wall). This is our NWL.
    \item \keyword{Mesh} -- a grid that \alert{bins} the measurement by location, so we get a wall \emph{map}, not just a total.
    \item \keyword{Vacuum boundary} -- an outer surface where a neutron that crosses is \alert{gone} (``leaks'' out and is counted).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize So: born in the plasma $\to$ free-stream across the void $\to$ cross a wall surface (counted by a meshed current tally) $\to$ leak out. Summing the bins gives the NWL map.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is OpenMC, and what is a ``custom source''?}
  \keyword{OpenMC} is a widely used, open-source Monte-Carlo neutron/photon transport code (C++ core, Python interface).
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}
    \item It ships with \alert{built-in sources} (point, isotropic, simple energy spectra).
    \item None of them know about \alert{spin-polarization directionality}. So we wrote a \keyword{custom (``compiled'') source}:
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
        \item a small C++ program that, each time OpenMC asks for a new neutron, returns its birth \alert{position, direction, energy};
        \item compiled into a plug-in library OpenMC loads at run time.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \alert{Our contribution lives almost entirely in that source} -- the rule that makes neutrons come out with the spin-polarized angular pattern.
\end{frame}

\section{Part 3 -- The physics of spin-polarized fusion}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Where does the directionality come from?}
  D and T nuclei have \keyword{spin} (a quantum ``orientation''). Normally the spins point every which way, and neutrons come off isotropically.
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Polarize} the fuel = line the spins up relative to the magnetic field $\Bhat$ \alert{before} they react.
    \item The fusion probability then depends on the \alert{angle $\theta$ between the outgoing neutron and $\Bhat$}.
    \item Result: neutrons preferentially come out \alert{across} or \alert{along} the field.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Intuition}
    Aligned spins give the reaction a ``preferred axis'' ($\Bhat$). The emission pattern is shaped relative to that axis -- that is the knob we exploit.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bookkeeping the spin states}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Deuteron} (spin 1): can be $m=+1,0,-1$ relative to $\Bhat$ -- fractions $d_+,d_0,d_-$.
    \item \keyword{Triton} (spin $\tfrac12$): $m=\pm\tfrac12$ -- fractions $t_+,t_-$.
  \end{itemize}
  The combinations collapse (by symmetry about $\Bhat$) into \alert{three ``collision modes''} $a,b,c$ (Schwartz Eq.~1):
  \[
    a = d_+t_+ + d_-t_-,\qquad b = d_0,\qquad c = d_+t_- + d_-t_+,\qquad a+b+c=1.
  \]
  \vfill
  \footnotesize \keyword{Unpolarized} fuel is the balanced mix $a=b=c=\tfrac13$. Pure modes ($a{=}1$, etc.) are idealized limits we use to isolate each effect.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The one equation that governs everything (Schwartz Eq.~2)}
  The chance of emitting a neutron at angle $\theta$ to the field:
  \[
    \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{\sigma_0}{2\pi}\Big[\;
      \underbrace{\tfrac34 a\,\sin^2\theta}_{\text{across }\Bhat}
      \;+\;\underbrace{\big(\tfrac23 b+\tfrac13 c\big)\big(\tfrac14+\tfrac34\cos^2\theta\big)}_{\text{along }\Bhat}\;\Big]
  \]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $d\sigma/d\Omega$ = relative number of neutrons per unit solid angle in direction $\theta$.
    \item $\sin^2\theta$ term peaks \alert{perpendicular} to $\Bhat$; $\tfrac14+\tfrac34\cos^2\theta$ term peaks \alert{parallel}.
    \item $\sigma_0$ is just an overall scale.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \alert{Read it as a recipe:} the fuel mix $(a,b,c)$ sets how much ``across'' vs.\ ``along'' emission you get.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two numbers actually matter for direction}
  Group the equation into two \alert{angular shapes} with two weights:
  \[
    w_\perp = \tfrac34 a \quad(\sin^2\theta),\qquad
    w_\parallel = \tfrac23 b + \tfrac13 c \quad(\tfrac14+\tfrac34\cos^2\theta).
  \]
  The birth-direction pattern depends \alert{only} on $(w_\perp,w_\parallel)$.
  \vfill
  Separately, the \alert{total number} of neutrons (the rate) scales with
  \[
    \eta = a + \tfrac23 b + \tfrac13 c \qquad(\text{``total-rate factor''}).
  \]
  \begin{block}{Why this split matters}
    \alert{Direction and rate are different questions.} Keeping them separate is the single most important modeling choice -- it lets us compare ``shapes'' at \alert{equal total fusion power}.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The three pure modes (our test cases)}
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{lccl}
    \toprule
    mode & $(a,b,c)$ & rate $\eta$ & emission pattern \\
    \midrule
    \keyword{unpolarized} & $(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$ & $2/3$ & \alert{isotropic} (flat) \\
    \keyword{A} & $(1,0,0)$ & $1$ ($+50\%$) & $\sin^2\theta$: \alert{across} $\Bhat$ (peaks $\perp$) \\
    \keyword{B} & $(0,1,0)$ & $2/3$ & $\tfrac14+\tfrac34\cos^2\theta$: \alert{along} $\Bhat$ \\
    \keyword{C} & $(0,0,1)$ & $1/3$ ($-50\%$) & \alert{same shape as B} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{A correction worth flagging}
    It is tempting to call mode C ``isotropic.'' \alert{It is not.} B and C share the \emph{same} angular shape; they differ only in how \emph{many} neutrons (the rate). We will see this confirmed both analytically and in OpenMC.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Energy: deliberately kept simple}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Real DT neutrons are born near 14.1 MeV with a small thermal spread.
    \item The spread is \alert{decoupled} from the angular (steering) physics, and irrelevant to the geometry of where neutrons land.
    \item So throughout the verification we use a single energy, \alert{14.1 MeV} -- exactly as Schwartz does.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize This keeps the comparison clean: any difference between OpenMC and analytic is about \alert{geometry/direction}, not energy.
\end{frame}

\section{Part 4 -- The analytic wall load (Schwartz 2025)}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{From emission pattern to wall load}
  Given how a source emits, the load on a wall patch is a classic geometry calculation:
  \[
    Q^{\text{NWL}}(\mathbf r_w)\;\propto\;\int_{\text{source}} \!\!
      \underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathbf r_s-\mathbf r_w|^2}}_{\text{inverse-square}}\;
      \underbrace{\cos\nu}_{\text{angle onto wall}}\;
      \underbrace{f(\theta)}_{\text{emission pattern}}\; dV
  \]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Inverse-square}: farther source points contribute less.
    \item \keyword{$\cos\nu$}: a glancing hit deposits less per area than a head-on hit.
    \item \keyword{$f(\theta)$}: the spin-polarization pattern (1 for isotropic, $\sin^2\theta$ for A, $\tfrac14+\tfrac34\cos^2\theta$ for B/C).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \alert{No scattering appears} -- this is the free-streaming, sightline picture.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Modeling the plasma as rings}
  An axisymmetric (doughnut-shaped) plasma is built from \keyword{circular ring} sources at major radius $p$, height $z$.
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.6\linewidth}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item Each wall target sees the ring from a range of toroidal angles $\varphi$.
        \item $|\Delta|$ = distance from a ring point to the target.
        \item $\cos\nu$ = how square-on the neutron hits the wall.
        \item $\cos\theta$ = angle of that sightline to the local field $\Bhat$ (this is where polarization enters).
        \item Integrate over the \alert{visible arc} of the ring.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.4\linewidth}
      \begin{block}{Plasma profile}
        \footnotesize A real plasma is denser in the middle. We weight the rings by a \alert{parabolic} $1-\rho^2$ profile ($\rho$ = normalized minor radius) and sum.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Schwartz's result: a fast closed form}
  Doing the ring integral yields \alert{closed-form expressions} (using elliptic integrals $F,E$), e.g.\ on the inboard wall:
  \[\resizebox{0.96\linewidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
    g_{RiI}=\frac{4p\sqrt{(p^2-u^2)(p^2-u^2+z^2)}}{q}
    -\frac{2p\,F(\varphi,m)}{u\sqrt{(p-u)^2+z^2}}
    +\frac{2p(p^2-u^2+z^2)\sqrt{(p-u)^2+z^2}}{q\,u}\,E(\varphi,m)
  $}\]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \alert{Instant} to evaluate and \alert{differentiable} $\Rightarrow$ usable inside design optimizers.
    \item Implemented in Schwartz's open-source \keyword{\texttt{anarr\'ima}} package -- our analytic ``ground truth.''
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize You do not need to read the formula -- the point is that an \alert{exact, independent} answer exists to check our simulation against.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Schwartz's headline example (what we reproduce)}
  A \keyword{square-cross-section torus}: circular plasma, four flat walls, purely toroidal field. Holding total fusion power fixed and comparing emission patterns:
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    quantity & effect \\
    \midrule
    unpolarized: outboard vs.\ inboard wall & outboard $\sim$12\% hotter (pure geometry) \\
    \keyword{A} mode at the inboard midplane & $+43\%$ \\
    \keyword{A} mode at the outboard midplane & $-22\%$ \\
    \keyword{B/C} mode & the mirror image ($-43\%$ inboard, $+22\%$ outboard) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \alert{Physics story:} B/C steers neutrons \alert{off the inboard center stack} and onto the outboard wall -- exactly what you want to protect the precious center column.
\end{frame}

\section{Part 5 -- What we built, and how we checked it}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{The goal, restated plainly}
  \begin{block}{Goal}
    Build a \alert{spin-polarized neutron source for OpenMC}, and \alert{prove it is correct} by recovering Schwartz's analytic results -- creating a trustworthy, transport-capable tool to go beyond what the analytic theory can do.
  \end{block}
  \vfill
  We worked in strict \keyword{tiers}, not advancing until each was verified:
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \keyword{Tier 0} -- get OpenMC built and the plug-in mechanism working.
    \item \keyword{Tier 1} -- the emission \emph{sampler} alone (no transport): are the directions right?
    \item \keyword{Tier 2a} -- our \emph{own} analytic calculation, to double-check the reference.
    \item \keyword{Tier 2b} -- the full OpenMC run: does it \alert{recover} the analytic?
    \item \keyword{Tier 3} -- turn the validated results into engineering numbers and figures.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 1 -- the ``sampler'': how a neutron's direction is drawn}
  Each time OpenMC needs a neutron, the source must \alert{randomly pick a direction} from the spin-polarized pattern. Three pieces:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Choose the shape} (``stratified sampling''): with probability $a/\eta$ use the $\sin^2\theta$ pattern, else the $\tfrac14+\tfrac34\cos^2\theta$ pattern.
    \item \keyword{Draw the angle} from that shape using an exact formula (inverse of its cumulative distribution).
    \item \keyword{Rotate} that direction so ``up'' aligns with the \alert{local field $\Bhat$} at the birth point.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Why a separate ``sampler'' tier?}
    Directions are the whole ballgame. We test them in isolation -- against exact math and a second method -- \alert{before} ever running a transport simulation.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 1 -- how we made the directions trustworthy}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Exact formula vs.\ a second method:} the angle is drawn by an exact inverse-formula, cross-checked against an independent ``accept/reject'' method -- they must give the same distribution.
    \item \keyword{C++ vs.\ Python twins:} we wrote the sampler twice (the real C++ and a Python mirror) and fed them the \alert{same random numbers}; outputs must match to machine precision -- this catches any typo.
    \item \keyword{Robust rotation:} the ``align with $\Bhat$'' step uses a method that never breaks, even when $\Bhat$ points straight up (a classic failure case).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize Plus: the source has \alert{no hidden state} and uses only OpenMC's random-number stream, so it is safe to run on many CPU cores at once.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 2b -- the OpenMC model (the actual simulation)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{Geometry}: a square-cross-section torus (matching Schwartz exactly), interior set to \alert{void} $\Rightarrow$ neutrons free-stream.
    \item \keyword{Source}: our compiled spin-polarized source, with the parabolic plasma profile and toroidal field.
    \item \keyword{Measurement}: a \alert{surface-current tally on a mesh} -- we count neutrons crossing each wall, binned into $\ge 50$ patches per wall, giving the NWL map.
    \item \keyword{Runs}: the unpolarized case and each pure mode (A, B, C), plus a mixture.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Sanity check built in}
    With a void interior every neutron must hit a wall exactly once. We confirmed the total counted current $=1.0000$ per neutron -- nothing lost, nothing double-counted.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How we compare OpenMC to the analytic (the ``recovery'')}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item For each wall patch we have \alert{two numbers}: the OpenMC count (with its statistical error bar) and the analytic value.
    \item We compare the \alert{directionality} (each mode \emph{relative to} unpolarized) -- this cancels overall scale and isolates the physics.
    \item The headline metric is the \keyword{standardized residual}:
      \[
        \frac{(\text{OpenMC} - \text{analytic})}{\text{statistical error}} .
      \]
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{What ``success'' looks like}
    These residuals should be \alert{random scatter centered on zero with spread $\approx 1$} (a standard bell curve). A consistent offset would mean a \alert{bug}; pure scatter means \alert{OpenMC reproduces the analytic to within noise}.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Part 6 -- Results (free-streaming tiers)}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Tier 1 result -- the sampler is correct}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{C++ vs.\ Python twins} agree to $\mathbf{6\times10^{-16}}$ (machine precision) -- no transcription errors.
    \item \keyword{Distributions match theory}: the drawn $\cos\theta$ histograms sit on the analytic curves (statistical test passes for every mode).
    \item \keyword{Steering is correct}: average ``alignment'' $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle$ = $0.20$ (A, across), $0.47$ (B/C, along), $0.33$ (isotropic) -- all reproduced.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize Plain reading: the source draws directions from \alert{exactly} the intended spin-polarized pattern.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 1 result -- seeing the patterns}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{tier1_costheta_per_mode.png}
  \end{center}

\begin{frame}{Tier 2a result -- our analytic matches the reference}
  Before trusting any single calculation, we computed the analytic wall load \alert{three independent ways}: our own numerical integral, Schwartz's \texttt{anarr\'ima}, and our own transcription of his closed form.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item All three agree to $\mathbf{\sim 10^{-15}}$ (machine precision) for a single ring.
    \item For the full parabolic plasma, our integral and \texttt{anarr\'ima} agree to $\mathbf{\sim 10^{-8}}$.
    \item We reproduce Schwartz's Figure 2 numbers (A: $+41\%$ inboard, $-21\%$ outboard).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize Why bother? So that when OpenMC is compared to ``the analytic,'' we are certain the analytic itself is right.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What ``recovery'' bought us (why this slide matters)}
  \begin{block}{The central result}
    In the no-scattering limit, the OpenMC spin-polarized source \alert{reproduces the exact analytic wall load to within statistical noise} -- across all four walls and all polarization modes.
  \end{block}
  \vfill
  Concretely, OpenMC independently confirms:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item the steering magnitudes (A: $+39\%$ inboard / $-21\%$ outboard; B/C the mirror);
    \item that \keyword{B and C are identical} (not C $=$ isotropic);
    \item \keyword{linearity}: a fuel mixture's pattern is the weighted sum of the pure-mode patterns.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill

\begin{frame}{Tier 3 -- turning it into engineering numbers}
  Holding total fusion power fixed, per mode:
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
     & unpolarized & A & B/C \\
    \midrule
    inboard (center-stack) peak vs.\ iso & --- & $+40\%$ & $\mathbf{-41\%}$ \\
    outboard peak vs.\ iso & --- & $-21\%$ & $+21\%$ \\
    fraction of all neutrons hitting inboard & $8.7\%$ & $12.3\%$ & $\mathbf{5.1\%}$ \\
    overall peaking factor (peak/avg) & $1.33$ & $1.65$ & $1.61$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{The good news (the design payoff)}
    B/C polarization \alert{moves $\sim$3.6\% of all wall neutrons off the inboard center stack} (8.7\%$\to$5.1\%) and onto the robust outboard wall, and cuts the center-stack peak load by $\sim$41\% -- at \alert{the same fusion power}.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An honest, slightly counter-intuitive finding}
  \alert{Polarization does not lower the \emph{overall} peaking factor here} -- it actually raises it (1.33 $\to$ 1.61).
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Why -- and why it's fine}
    Any directional emission \emph{concentrates} the load, so the most uniform case is the boring isotropic one. The benefit of B/C is \alert{not} a flatter wall overall; it is \alert{specifically protecting the center stack}. Schwartz's flatter-wall result comes from \emph{then re-shaping the wall} to exploit the steering -- which we did not model.
  \end{block}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize We report this plainly rather than imply a benefit the model doesn't show -- correctness of interpretation matters as much as the numbers.
\end{frame}

\section{Part 7 -- Beyond free-streaming: scattering, breeding, the rate}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Turning on the real physics}
  Everything so far was \alert{free-streaming} (geometry only), checked against the exact analytic answer. Now we give the wall \alert{real materials}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item neutrons \keyword{scatter}, deposit \keyword{heat}, cause \keyword{damage}, and \keyword{breed tritium} -- the things the analytic \emph{cannot} do.
    \item the catch: \alert{no closed-form answer any more}, so each new step needs a fresh way to stay honest (limiting cases, conservation, benchmarks).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{The reactor model (paper-grounded: ARC / Peterson's LIBRA)}
    A layered box-torus: \keyword{tungsten} first wall (1 mm) $\to$ steel $\to$ \keyword{beryllium} multiplier $\to$ \keyword{FLiBe} breeder blanket (40 cm), at ARC scale. Same compiled spin-polarized source inside.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 5 -- what scattering changes, and staying honest}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
      \begin{block}{New things we can now measure}
        \footnotesize wall \keyword{heating} (MW/m$^2$), material \keyword{damage} (dpa), the neutron \keyword{energy spectrum}, and \keyword{tritium}.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\linewidth}
      \begin{block}{The anchor (no analytic now)}
        \footnotesize Make the walls nearly transparent (density $\div 10^4$) $\to$ neutrons free-stream again $\to$ must \alert{recover Tier 2b}.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \keyword{Anchor result:} in the thin-wall limit the steering returns to
  \alert{A $+40\%$ inboard, B/C $-40\%$} -- exactly the validated free-streaming
  values. So the full layered model + source are correct.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 5 -- the key new finding: scattering \alert{dilutes} the steering}
  With the \emph{real} blanket, neutrons bounce back out of the wall as a diffuse, direction-blind glow that washes out the directional contrast:
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    inboard (center stack) & free-streaming & \alert{with scattering} \\
    \midrule
    A mode & $+40\%$ & $\mathbf{+13\%}$ \\
    B/C mode & $-40\%$ & $\mathbf{-15\%}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Why this matters}
    \alert{Free-streaming overestimates the center-stack benefit by $\sim$3$\times$.} B/C still protects the center stack, but by $\sim$15\%, not 41\% -- a correction \emph{only} the transport model can give.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 5 -- where the energy goes, and the scattering fingerprint}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{tier5_heating_spectrum.png}
  \end{center}
  \footnotesize Left: heating splits -- the \alert{FLiBe blanket takes $\sim$91\%}, the thin tungsten first wall only $\sim$0.1\%. Right: the FLiBe flux shows the 14.1 MeV source peak \alert{plus a large down-scattered tail} to keV energies -- the direct proof scattering is active, and what drives low-energy breeding and damage.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 6 -- why a breeder blanket? (tritium self-sufficiency)}
  DT fusion \emph{burns} tritium, which barely exists in nature (12-year half-life). \alert{A reactor must make its own.}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A \keyword{lithium blanket} does it: $n + {}^6\mathrm{Li} \to {}^4\mathrm{He} + \mathrm{T}$.
    \item Metric: \keyword{TBR} (tritium breeding ratio) = tritium made per fusion neutron. Need \alert{TBR $\gtrsim 1$} to be self-sufficient.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{Our blanket = FLiBe (after ARC / Peterson's LIBRA)}
    \footnotesize A molten Li--Be--F salt (Li$_2$BeF$_4$), $^6$Li-enriched, with a beryllium neutron multiplier -- exactly the molten-salt immersion concept your group studies.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 6 -- the TBR result}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{tier6_tbr.png}
  \end{center}
  \footnotesize \alert{TBR = 1.15} (in the ARC/LIBRA band, at the DEMO goal). \alert{Left:} TBR rises with $^6$Li enrichment then \emph{peaks near 50\%} -- reproducing the published ``20--50\% optimal'' result (a real verification). \alert{Right:} TBR is \alert{nearly the same for every polarization mode} ($\pm0.6\%$): \textbf{B/C protects the center stack without sacrificing breeding} -- the clean design message.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 7 -- restoring the rate (the famous +50\%)}
  Until now we held the fusion \emph{rate} fixed, to isolate steering. But aligning spins \alert{also boosts the reaction rate} -- and that was in our model all along, as the factor $\eta = a + \tfrac23 b + \tfrac13 c$:
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    mode & $\eta$ & fusion rate vs unpolarized \\
    \midrule
    unpolarized & 2/3 & --- \\
    \alert{A} & 1 & \alert{$+50\%$} \\
    B & 2/3 & 0 \\
    \alert{C} & 1/3 & \alert{$-50\%$} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize At fixed fuel density the neutron yield scales with $\eta$ -- so we just multiply the per-neutron results by it (pure postprocessing, no new runs).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tier 7 -- the rate-vs-steering trade-off}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\linewidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{tier7_absolute.png}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
      \footnotesize (all relative to unpolarized fuel = 1)
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
        \item \keyword{A:} $1.5\times$ power \& tritium -- but $1.7\times$ center-stack load (rate \emph{compounds} the inboard steering). \alert{Power play; hard on the center stack.}
        \item \keyword{B:} same power, $0.85\times$ center stack -- \alert{free steering}, the sweet spot.
        \item \keyword{C:} $0.5\times$ everything -- lowest center-stack, but half the power.
        \item \alert{TBR per neutron is unchanged} $\Rightarrow$ the rate boost breeds proportionally more; self-sufficiency is preserved.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize \alert{Tension:} the rate boost (A) and the center-stack protection (B/C) are \emph{different spin states} -- you cannot maximize both. B uniquely buys steering at no rate cost.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the big SPF benefits actually live}
  The headline spin-polarized-fuel claims span \alert{three different physics layers}; we model only the last:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \keyword{$+50\%$ cross section} -- \alert{in our model} (the factor $\eta$, Tier 7).
    \item \keyword{$+70$--$80\%$ net electricity, lower density} -- a plasma + \alert{systems} power-balance result (gain $Q$, recirculating power) -- \emph{upstream} of neutron transport.
    \item \keyword{$\sim$10$\times$ tritium burn efficiency / lower inventory} -- a plasma \alert{fuel-cycle} effect (how much injected T burns) -- \emph{distinct} from our blanket TBR (breeding \emph{supply} vs burn \emph{demand}).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{The honest framing}
    Our code is the \alert{neutron-transport block}: source $\to$ wall $\to$ blanket. The other benefits couple in from plasma and systems codes -- a natural next integration, not something a transport tally can show.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Part 8 -- Scope, novelty, and outlook}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{The full model now -- what it is, and its scoping limits}
  \begin{block}{It now IS}
    A transport model with \alert{scattering}, a \alert{tungsten first wall} (heating/damage/spectrum), and a \alert{FLiBe breeder blanket} (a real TBR) -- plus the \alert{rate} effect.
  \end{block}
  \begin{block}{Still a scoping model (deliberate, for verifiability)}
    \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
      \item purely \alert{toroidal} field; single-zone blanket; \alert{no depletion}/burnup, no coolant or tritium-extraction detail;
      \item \alert{square} cross-section (not ARC's D-shape), no divertor; neutron channel only; 294 K data with 900 K density.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this sits in the literature}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The spin-polarized cross section is \alert{old physics}; the \alert{analytic wall load} is new (Schwartz 2025) -- and explicitly \alert{free-streaming}.
    \item Mainstream fusion neutronics uses \alert{isotropic} sources; a \alert{Monte-Carlo transport} treatment of the polarized emission appears to be \alert{open ground} (worth confirming with experts).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{block}{So what's the value of our work?}
    The reproduction \alert{validates the machinery}; the new results are the steps the analytic theory \emph{cannot} take -- which our verified source \alert{now delivers} (Part 7): scattering dilutes the steering, a real TBR, and the rate trade-off.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Next steps (scattering + blanket now done)}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \keyword{Realistic / angled field} (next up): couple a real equilibrium field / \alert{Peterson's physics-informed plasma source}. The sampler already handles any field direction and \texttt{anarr\'ima} has the matching angled analytic, so the free-streaming check survives.
    \item \keyword{Depletion \& multi-zone blanket}: $^6$Li burnup, activation, coolant channels -- a time-dependent, more realistic TBR.
    \item \keyword{Realistic geometry}: D-shaped cross-section, divertor (drop the square-section simplification).
    \item \keyword{Couple to plasma + systems codes}: capture the \emph{upstream} SPF benefits -- net electricity / $Q$ and tritium burn-efficiency -- with our neutronics as one block in the loop.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Spin polarization \alert{steers DT neutrons} (via the cross section's angle to $\hat B$) and \alert{boosts the rate} -- two effects in different spin states.
    \item We built a spin-polarized OpenMC source and \alert{verified it}: sampler (machine-precision), independent analytic ($10^{-8}$), and OpenMC \alert{recovers the analytic} to within noise.
    \item \keyword{Free-streaming:} B/C cuts the geometric center-stack load (Tier 3). \keyword{With scattering:} backscatter \alert{dilutes that benefit $\sim$3$\times$} (to $\sim$15\%) -- the analytic was optimistic.
    \item \keyword{Blanket:} \alert{TBR = 1.15} (ARC/LIBRA band), and \alert{steering barely changes it} -- protect the center stack without costing breeding.
    \item \keyword{Rate:} A mode $+50\%$ power (and tritium), but compounds the center-stack load; B is the steering sweet spot. The bigger SPF gains (net electricity, burn efficiency) live upstream in plasma/systems physics.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
